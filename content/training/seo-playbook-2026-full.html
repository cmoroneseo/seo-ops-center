The SEO playbook: universal and local fundamentals, graded by evidence

Almost everything written about SEO is asserted rather than demonstrated. This playbook separates what Google documents, what internal documents and sworn testimony confirm, what large-scale data merely correlates with, and what is folklore — across both organic search and local search — then turns the durable parts into a checklist you can work through.

How to read this

This playbook covers two related but mechanically different disciplines. Part One is universal search — ranking web pages in Google's organic results. Part Two is local search — ranking a business entity in the map pack and localised results. Local sits on top of universal: everything in Part One still applies to a local business's website, and none of Part Two substitutes for it.

Use the track chips to show only the half you're working in, and the evidence chips to hide anything below your tolerance for uncertainty. Every checklist item carries an evidence grade. The grade says how much weight the claim can bear, not how important it is.

A  Documented

Stated by Google in its developer documentation, Business Profile Help, Maps policies, or its own published playbooks. In the local half these double as compliance rules — breaking them removes the listing, not just the ranking.

B  Corroborated

Supported by the May 2024 Content Warehouse API leak, sworn testimony in US v. Google, or Google's own published performance figures. Real internal artefacts — partly stale, stripped of weighting.

C  Studied

Large-N observational studies, SERP tracking, and structured expert surveys (Whitespark's 47-specialist panel scoring 187 local factors; BrightLocal's consumer research). Measured — but measuring observation or opinion, not mechanism.

D  Practitioner

Consistent, replicated field experience without formal evidence. Worth doing when cheap and harmless; worth dropping the moment it costs real time. This tier is larger in local, where the algorithm is less documented and more testable.

Two honest caveatsGoogle runs hundreds of signals, re-ranks after the fact, and personalises by location, language and device — no public document reveals weights, and anyone quoting a percentage for a "ranking factor" invented it. And in local, proximity means there is no single ranking at all: your position changes street by street, so "we rank #1" is incomplete without a location.

Fundamentals, strategy, roadmap

Three words that get used interchangeably and shouldn't be. Everything in this playbook is fundamentals — the standard. Strategy and roadmap are what turn that standard into work for a specific client. Confusing them is the reason onboarding feels like starting from scratch every time.

Fundamentals

The standard. Identical for every client.
Answers: what must be true of any site we touch? A quality floor, enforced rather than debated. Nobody buys this line item; it's what stops us shipping strategy onto a broken base.

Strategy

The choices unique to this client.
Answers: given this business, where does effort turn into revenue fastest, and what are we deliberately not doing? Mostly a diagnosis plus a refusal. Fits on one page.

Roadmap

Strategy on a calendar, under real constraints.
Answers: what, in what order, by when, by whom? Shaped as much by hours, budget, dev access and client approval speed as by strategy.

The sequence

The intuitive version — "fundamentals plus a tailored strategy equals a custom roadmap" — is close, with two corrections. Fundamentals aren't an input to strategy; they run underneath regardless of it. We don't decide whether to fix indexation based on strategy. And there's a missing step in the middle:

The actual flowFundamentals (fixed) → Diagnosis (fixed process, variable output) → Strategy (3–5 decisions) → Roadmap (assembled, not written)

Diagnosis is the step most often skipped, and it's the one that makes strategy fast. Every client has one binding constraint — the thing that, until fixed, makes everything else not work. Name it and the sequence largely picks itself.

What's fixed and what's invented

The bottleneck in onboarding is inventing what should be selected. Customisation means creating something new; configuration means choosing from known options. Agencies scale on configuration. Roughly this is the split we're aiming for:

Once those exist, a roadmap stops being a document you write and becomes plays selected from the library, ordered by dependency, fitted to the retainer's monthly hours. Onboarding becomes deviations from a default rather than a blank page. If a new client needs more than a day of strategic thinking, either the diagnostic is weak or we're missing an archetype.

The strategy one-pager

Six headings, one page, no exceptions:

  • Constraint — the binding constraint, named, with the evidence for it.
  • Where we compete — which services, which geography, how tight.
  • What we're refusing — written down explicitly. This is the half that protects us later.
  • The bet — one sentence on why this converts faster than the alternatives.
  • Success at 90 days — in business terms, not positions.
  • What we need from the client — access, approvals, information, decisions.

The mental model · universal search

Universal SEO has three jobs, in order. Everything else is a tactic serving one of them.

SEO has exactly three jobs, in this order. Everything else is a tactic serving one of them. If a proposed task doesn't map onto one of the three, it is probably decoration.

1

Be reachable

Google must be able to fetch the page, render it as a user would, and choose it as the canonical version of its content. A page that fails here cannot rank at any quality level.

2

Be the best answer

The page must satisfy a real query better than the alternatives currently ranking — measured by usefulness to the person, not by keyword coverage.

3

Be credible

The site must carry enough independent corroboration — links, citations, brand demand, real-world reputation — that Google will risk showing it.

Google's own description of Search is three stages: crawling, indexing, and serving. At the indexing stage it groups near-identical pages and "select[s] the one that's most representative of the group." At the serving stage, "hundreds of factors" decide relevance, modified by the searcher's location, language and device. The practical consequence is that ranking is a relative, contextual judgement — you are never optimising in the abstract, only against the ten results currently on the page.

What changed, and what didn'tBetween 2024 and 2026 the surface of search changed enormously — AI Overviews appeared on roughly a quarter of queries by Q1 2026, and click-through rates on position one fell sharply. The inputs changed very little. The same crawlability, content quality, relevance and authority signals that decided blue-link rankings also decide which pages get quoted in generated answers. Fundamentals didn't stop working; the payout per ranking dropped.

Pillar 1 · Demand and intent

Ranking is worthless if nobody searches for the thing, and impossible if your page answers a different question than the one being asked. Demand research and intent matching come first because they determine whether the other six pillars have anything to work on.

What the data says about search demand

Search demand is brutally long-tailed. Ahrefs found that 94.74% of keywords get ten or fewer searches a month, while only 0.0008% exceed 100,000. On the supply side, 96.55% of pages get zero organic traffic from Google, and only 1.94% get between one and ten visits a month. Those two facts together explain most SEO disappointment: publishing more pages is not a strategy, because the median page earns nothing.

Intent is inferred from the SERP, not from the keyword

Google's ranking systems named in its own documentation — RankBrain, neural matching, BERT and the passage ranking system — all exist to map concepts rather than strings. Neural matching "matches concepts in queries to representations found on pages"; passage ranking "analyzes individual page sections to assess relevance." The operational takeaway is that you cannot argue with a SERP: whatever page type currently occupies the top ten is Google's judgement about intent for that query. If the results are all comparison tables and you publish an essay, you have chosen to lose.

The site diversity systemGoogle documents a system that "limits top results to generally two listings per site." Planning five pages to blanket one SERP is planning for three of them to be suppressed. Consolidate instead.
Demand & intent checklist
Validate demand before writing C
Confirm the query has non-trivial volume or clear commercial value. Given that 94.7% of keywords see ≤10 searches/month, cluster related long-tail queries into one page rather than chasing them individually.
Read the SERP before choosing a format A
Note the dominant page type, the presence of AI Overviews, and which SERP features occupy space. Match the format Google is already rewarding rather than the one you prefer.
One dominant intent per URL D
Splitting one intent across several pages creates cannibalisation; merging several intents into one page dilutes relevance for all of them.

Size the opportunity against realistic timelines C
Only ~5.7% of new pages reach the top 10 within a year, and 72.9% of current top-10 pages are over three years old. Budget for compounding, not for quarterly wins.
Map queries to a business outcome D
Rank each target by proximity to revenue, not by volume. High-volume informational terms are increasingly answered inside AI Overviews without a click.

Pillar 2 · Crawling and indexing

This is the only pillar with a hard pass/fail. Every technical SEO problem worth fixing eventually expresses itself as: Google can't fetch it, can't render it, or picked a different URL than you intended.

The three stages, and what breaks at each

Google names three stages. Crawling fails on server errors, network problems, login walls, and "robots.txt rules preventing Googlebot's access to the page." Indexing fails on low content quality, robots noindex rules, page designs that hinder analysis, and duplicate or non-canonical status. Serving is where relevance is decided. Note the ordering trap that catches people constantly: robots.txt blocks crawling, not indexing — a URL blocked in robots.txt can still appear in results, and a noindex tag on a robots-blocked page will never be read.

Canonicalisation, ranked by strength

When you don't specify, "Google will identify which version of the URL is objectively the best version to show to users in Search." Your influence over that decision runs, strongest to weakest: permanent redirects → rel="canonical" (HTML head or HTTP header) → XML sitemaps. Google also states it "prefers HTTPS pages over equivalent HTTP pages as canonical." Consistent internal linking is the quiet fourth signal; inconsistent internal links actively undermine your stated preference.

Four canonical mistakes Google calls outUsing robots.txt or the removal tool for canonicalisation; specifying different canonicals for the same page through different methods; using a URL fragment as a canonical; and using noindex to steer canonical selection within a site.

Render parity

Google's guidance is explicit: "When Google crawls a page, it should ideally see the page the same way an average user does." That single sentence is the whole of JavaScript SEO. If the main content, internal links or structured data only exist after a client-side fetch that Googlebot doesn't execute, the page is effectively empty. The URL Inspection tool in Search Console shows the rendered HTML Google actually holds — this is the check, not a browser view-source.

From the leaked documentationThe Content Warehouse schema includes hostAge, described as being used "to sandbox fresh spam in serving time." This is consistent with the long-observed pattern that brand-new hosts underperform for a period regardless of content quality — plan for a ramp, and don't diagnose a new domain's slow start as a technical fault.
Crawl & index checklist
Confirm indexation coverage in Search Console A
Work the Page Indexing report by reason. "Crawled – currently not indexed" and "Discovered – currently not indexed" at scale are quality and crawl-priority signals, not bugs to be forced.
Verify rendered HTML for your top templates A
Use URL Inspection → Test Live URL → View Crawled Page on one URL per template. Check that body content, internal links and structured data are present.
Never block a page you want de-indexed A
Use noindex on a crawlable URL, or a 410/404. Robots.txt disallow prevents Google from ever seeing the noindex directive.
Self-referencing canonical on every indexable page A
Absolute URL, matching the version you link to internally, matching the version in your sitemap, on HTTPS.
One reachable URL per piece of content A
Redirect www/non-www, http/https, trailing-slash and index-file variants. Strip or canonicalise tracking and session parameters.
Keep the XML sitemap clean and truthful A
Canonical, indexable, 200-status URLs only, with accurate lastmod. Sitemaps are optional for small sites but valuable for large, new or poorly-linked ones.
Contain crawl traps D
Faceted navigation, calendars, internal search results and infinite pagination generate unbounded URL space. Block, canonicalise or nofollow them deliberately rather than letting them accumulate.
Fix server reliability before anything else A
Sustained 5xx errors and slow responses suppress crawl rate. Check the Crawl Stats report for response-time trends and error spikes.
Preserve equity through migrations D
Map every old URL to a single relevant new URL with a 301. Chains, loops and blanket redirects to the homepage are the standard causes of post-migration collapse.

Pillar 3 · Content quality

This is where core updates are won and lost, and it is the pillar most resistant to shortcuts. Google's public position and its internal machinery agree more here than anywhere else.

People-first, defined by Google

Google defines people-first content as material "created primarily for people, and not to manipulate search engine rankings," and lists the tells of the opposite: producing "lots of content on many different topics" hoping some sticks, "extensive automation to produce content on many topics," "mainly summarizing what others have to say without adding much value," writing for trends rather than for an audience, and changing dates to look fresh "when the content has not substantially changed."

The self-assessment questions worth actually running

  • Does the content provide original information, reporting, research or analysis?
  • Does it provide "insightful analysis or interesting information that is beyond the obvious"?
  • Is this "the sort of page you'd want to bookmark, share with a friend, or recommend"?
  • Does it present information "in a way that makes you want to trust it, such as clear sourcing, evidence of the expertise involved"?
  • Was it produced by "an expert or enthusiast who demonstrably knows the topic well"?
  • Does it contain "easily-verified factual errors"?

Who, How and Why

Who made it — bylines and author pages that demonstrate real background. How it was made — for reviews, "the number of products that were tested, what the test results were, and how the tests were conducted"; for AI-assisted work, disclosure where "someone might think 'How was this created?'". Why it exists — the load-bearing question. Content should exist "primarily to help people." Using automation "to produce content for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings" is a spam policy violation, not a grey area.

E-E-A-T, precisely

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust — with trust the most important. Google is equally explicit that E-E-A-T is not itself a ranking factor: asked directly, the answer is "No, it's not." Both things are true at once. E-E-A-T is the concept quality raters are trained to evaluate; the ranking systems approximate it through many separate signals. The leaked schema is consistent with that — it contains ymylHealthScore and ymylNewsScore classifiers, an originalContentScore, and site-level quality attributes, but nothing called "EEAT."

March 2026 core update, in the aggregateIndependent SERP tracking found 79.5% of top-3 positions and 90.7% of top-10 positions changed, with ~24.1% of top-10 pages falling out of the top 100 entirely. The directional finding: rankings shifted "from intermediary sites toward stronger destination sources." Aggregators, directories, comparison sites and job boards lost; official institutions, established brands, specialist niche sources and primary-data holders gained. If your page's only reason to exist is that it summarises other people's pages, that is now the risk position.

Recovering from a core update

Google's guidance is unusually blunt: wait "at least a full week after a core update completes before analyzing your site"; a move from position 2 to 4 needs no action; and "avoid doing 'quick fix' changes (like removing some page element because you heard it was bad for SEO)." Deleting content is "a last resort," and the need to delete suggests the content "was created for search engines first, and not people." Recovery "can take effect in a few days, but it could take several months," with "no guarantee."

Decay is the default state

Published content loses traffic continuously — one analysis puts average decay at roughly 1.2% per week for ageing posts. The counter is a refresh discipline: statistics and screenshots updated, broken links fixed, gaps that competitors now cover filled, overlapping pages merged with redirects, and titles/meta rewritten. Refreshing an existing ranking URL is consistently cheaper than earning a new one, because the page already holds links, history and click data.

Content quality checklist
Add something that isn't on the other ten results A
Original data, first-hand testing, named expert commentary, photography you took, a calculator, a template. Synthesis alone is the profile that lost ground in 2026 core updates.
Real bylines with real credentials A
Author page, relevant background, links to their work elsewhere. Fictional personas are a trust liability, particularly on YMYL topics.
Show your method A
State how many things you tested, over what period, with what results. Google names this explicitly for review content.
Disclose AI involvement where a reader would wonder A
Google's stated bar: disclose "when it would be reasonably expected," and explain why automation was useful.
Do not publish at scale without per-page value A
Scaled content abuse covers "generating numerous pages without user value specifically to manipulate rankings" — regardless of whether a human or a model wrote them.
Guard against site reputation abuse A
Third-party content published on your domain to borrow its ranking strength is a named policy violation. Applies to coupon sections, sponsored subfolders and rented subdomains.
Keep dates honest A
Only change a "last updated" date when the content substantially changed. The leak shows Google extracting bylineDate, syntacticDate and semanticDate separately — inconsistent dates are detectable.

Improve or consolidate first. Delete only pages with no traffic, no links, no conversions and no strategic role — and redirect where a relevant target exists.

Pillar 4 · On-page relevance

On-page work is the cheapest, fastest, most reliably positive category of SEO — and the one most polluted by ritual. The rule of thumb: anything that helps a reader or clarifies the topic is worth doing; anything whose only audience is a crawler is not.

Titles

Google generates the title link from several sources — the <title> element, visible headings, <h1>, Open Graph tags, styled text, and anchor text pointing at the page. It rewrites titles when they are stale, inaccurate, half-empty (| Site Name alone), boilerplate across many pages, or keyword-stuffed ("foobar, foo bar, foobars"). There is no hard length limit; titles are "truncated in Google Search results as needed, typically to fit the device width." The leaked schema contains titlematchScore, "a signal that tells how well titles are matching user queries" — which is the strongest available support for the oldest on-page rule there is: put the actual subject of the page in the title.

Meta descriptions

Not a ranking input. They are advertising copy for the result, and Google frequently rewrites them from page text when its own extract fits the query better. Write "short, unique to one particular page" summaries that include the most relevant points — one or two sentences.

Headings, structure and placement

Google's position on heading order is that it doesn't matter "from Google Search perspective" and there is "no magical, ideal amount of headings." Headings still matter for readers, for accessibility, and for passage ranking, which evaluates sections independently. What does appear to matter mechanically is placement: leaked documentation indicates documents are truncated at a maximum token count, so content buried below thousands of words of preamble may not be considered at all. Answer the question near the top.

Structured data

Structured data is not a ranking factor; it is an eligibility mechanism for rich results, and rich results move CTR. Google's own case studies cite +25% CTR (Rotten Tomatoes), +35% visits (Food Network) and +82% CTR on rich-result pages (Nestlé). Two rules carry most of the risk: "don't add structured data about information that is not visible to the user, even if the information is accurate," and prefer "fewer but complete and accurate recommended properties" over exhaustive but sloppy markup.

Unique, descriptive title on every indexable page A
Primary subject first, brand last behind a delimiter. No repeated keyword variants, no shared boilerplate across templates.

Supported by titlematchScore in the leaked schema. Not keyword stuffing — literal, plain-language correspondence between the query and the title.
Check which titles Google is rewriting A
Rewrites usually mean a mismatch between your title and the page's visible content. Fix the page or the title rather than fighting the rewrite.
Distinct meta descriptions on pages that matter A
One or two sentences, most relevant points, written as ad copy. Skip on low-value templates rather than duplicating.
Front-load the answer B
Direct answer in the first 100–150 words, then depth. Serves passage ranking, featured snippets, AI-answer extraction and readers who bounce.
Use headings for structure, not for keyword placement A
Order is not a ranking factor. Clear, scannable, descriptive subheads that map to sub-questions are the goal.
Descriptive anchor text on internal and external links A
Google names "appropriate anchor text" as a way to help users and search engines understand the destination. "Click here" wastes a signal.
Images near relevant text, with real alt text A
Alt text should explain the image's relationship to the surrounding content, not restate the target keyword.

Article, Product, FAQ, Breadcrumb, Organization, LocalBusiness as applicable. Validate with the Rich Results Test and monitor the enhancement reports.
Readable URLs grouped in topical directories A
Google notes that directories help it "learn how often the URLs in individual directories change." Keyword-stuffed URLs add nothing; clear ones cost nothing.
No keyword stuffing anywhere A
A named spam policy: "filling a web page with keywords or numbers in an attempt to manipulate rankings." Includes hidden text, white-on-white, and off-screen positioning.

Pillar 5 · Architecture and internal links

Internal linking is the highest-leverage under-used lever in SEO: it is entirely under your control, costs nothing external, and directly governs how authority and crawl attention are distributed across your site.

How authority moves inside a site

Google names "Link analysis systems and PageRank" as an active ranking system that evaluates "how pages link to determine relevance and helpfulness." PageRank does not distinguish between internal and external links in principle — which means your own linking decides where accumulated authority lands. The leaked documentation reinforces the picture, containing homepage trust attributes (homePageInfo with values including "FULLY_TRUSTED") and site-level authority scoring, alongside the practitioner reading that homepage strength propagates outward through the link graph.

Topical cohesion is measured

Two leaked attributes are particularly instructive: siteFocusScore, "how much a site is focused on one topic," and siteRadius, "how far page_embeddings deviate from the site_embedding." Combined with pageEmbedding and siteEmbedding, this describes a system that models what a site is about and notices pages that sit far outside it. That is a mechanical basis for topical authority — build depth in a defined territory before spreading into adjacent ones.

Architecture checklist
Group related pages into directories A
A flat dump of URLs gives Google no structural information. Directories communicate grouping and update frequency.
Keep important pages within ~3 clicks of the homepage D
Deep pages are crawled less and receive less internal authority. Depth, not directory nesting, is what matters.
Eliminate orphan pages D
Crawl the site and diff against the sitemap. Any indexable URL with zero internal links is invisible to the link graph.
Point internal links at your money pages B
Identify the pages with the most external links and link from them, in-content, to the pages you actually need to rank.
Use varied, descriptive internal anchors A
Anchor text is one of the sources Google uses to understand a destination page — and one of the sources it uses to generate title links.
Build hub-and-spoke topic clusters B
A pillar page linking to and from every supporting page. Consistent with siteFocusScore and siteRadius modelling site-level topical coherence.
Resist expanding beyond your topic too early D
Off-topic sections dilute site-level focus and rarely rank on a site with no track record in that area.
Retire dead internal links D
Internal links to redirected or 404 URLs leak crawl budget and dilute the signal. Update the links at source.
Use crawlable <a href> links A
Google's own advice is to keep "links crawlable so that Google can find other pages on your site." JavaScript click handlers without href attributes are not links.

Pillar 6 · Off-site authority

Links remain the most durably confirmed off-site signal — and the area with the widest gap between what works and what is sold. The distinguishing question is whether a link exists because someone judged your page worth citing.

What is actually established

  • Google runs link analysis and PageRank as a named, current ranking system A — it is listed in Google's own guide to ranking systems.
  • Site-level authority exists internally B — the leaked schema contains siteAuthority, "converted from quality_nsr.SiteAuthority, applied in Qstar," despite years of public statements that Google has no domain authority metric. Third-party scores like DR and DA still aren't that metric; they merely approximate the same idea.
  • Link value is not uniform B — the leaked documentation describes source-page tiering and homepage trust states, supporting the long-standing view that one editorially-given link from a trusted, relevant page outweighs hundreds of low-tier ones.
  • Referring domains correlate with organic traffic C — consistently, across large studies, with higher-ranking pages also acquiring new referring domains faster. Correlation, and partly reverse causation: ranking earns links too.

What Google's spam policy actually prohibits

Link spam is "creating inbound or outbound links primarily for ranking manipulation, including paid links without proper attributes." The relevant machinery is attribution, not abstinence: rel="sponsored" for paid or affiliate links, rel="ugc" for user-generated links, rel="nofollow" for links you can't vouch for. Google's guidance is that user-generated links should have nofollow "automatically added by your CMS." Two adjacent policies catch common agency tactics: expired domain abuse (buying old domains to inherit their signals) and site reputation abuse (publishing third-party content on a strong host to exploit its rankings).

The reliable link sources, in rough order of returnOriginal research and proprietary data others want to cite · being the primary source for a piece of news in your niche · genuinely useful free tools · expert commentary offered to journalists · partnerships, sponsorships and community involvement that would exist without SEO · unlinked brand mentions converted to links. Everything on this list works because a human decided to link.

What the leak and the trial showed

Two events between 2023 and 2024 moved several long-argued questions from speculation to something closer to fact: sworn testimony in the US Department of Justice's antitrust case against Google, and the May 2024 accidental publication of Google's Content Warehouse API documentation. Neither gives weights. Both constrain what is plausible.

Click and engagement signals are used

The leaked schema names goodClicks, badClicks, lastLongestClicks, unsquashedClicks and unicornClicks, associated with the NavBoost re-ranking system, along with lastGoodClickDate tracking traffic decay. This sits directly against years of public statements that using clicks in ranking "would be a mistake."

Read this carefully before acting on itThese are search-result interaction signals — whether searchers clicked your result and whether the click ended their search — measured in aggregate across queries. They are not your Google Analytics bounce rate, and they are not something you improve by buying clicks. The legitimate implication is narrow and old-fashioned: earn the click with an honest, well-matched snippet, and satisfy the visit so the searcher doesn't go back and pick a competitor.

Site-level authority exists

siteAuthority appears in the schema, "converted from quality_nsr.SiteAuthority, applied in Qstar." Separately, hostAge is used "to sandbox fresh spam in serving time," smallPersonalSite allows discretionary treatment of independent blogs, and babyPandaV2Demotion sits "on top of Panda." The reasonable conclusion is not that Ahrefs DR or Moz DA are ranking factors — they are not — but that site-level quality and authority scoring is real, that new hosts face a probationary period, and that site-wide quality problems can demote individual pages that are fine on their own merits.

Content is modelled semantically, and truncated

pageEmbedding, siteEmbedding, siteFocusScore and siteRadius together describe topic modelling at both page and site level. originalContentScore measures originality specifically "for pages with little content." titlematchScore measures title-to-query correspondence. And documents are truncated at a maximum token count — a mundane engineering detail with a real content consequence: length beyond a point isn't merely useless, it is unread.

What the two sources do not establish

  • Weights. Nothing in either source says how much any attribute matters.
  • Currency. The documentation is a snapshot; attributes may be deprecated, experimental, or unused in the live serving path.
  • Direct manipulability. Knowing an attribute exists does not tell you how to move it, and most attempts to move engagement metrics artificially are detectable and pointless.

Used properly, these sources are a filter for advice: any tactic that would have looked implausible before the leak and still looks implausible after it can be discarded with more confidence, and any long-denied phenomenon that practitioners kept observing anyway — sandboxing, site-level quality, click-informed re-ranking — can be planned around.

How local search differs · two rankings, three factors

The single most common strategic error in local SEO is treating it as one ranking problem. It is two, and they respond to different inputs.

The local pack & Maps

Ranks Business Profiles, not web pages. Driven primarily by profile data, proximity, reviews and prominence. Your website influences it, but the entity being ranked is the listing.

Localised organic

Ranks web pages with a geographic modifier applied. Driven by everything in classic SEO — content, links, technical health — plus geographic relevance.

Whitespark's 2026 survey makes the split concrete. The top five factors experts scored for the local pack were: primary GBP category, proximity of address to the point of search, keywords in the GBP business title, physical address in the city of search, and whether the business is open at the time of search. The top five for local organic were entirely different: a dedicated page for each service, geographic keyword relevance of content, quality and authority of inbound links to the domain, keywords in the GBP landing page title tag, and quantity of industry-relevant inbound links.

Google's three stated factors

Google's own explanation is short enough to quote in full. Relevance is "how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for." Distance is "how far each business is from the customer who's searching." Prominence is "how well-known a business is," based on factors including "websites link to your business and how many reviews you have." And the line that ends most sales conversations: "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google."

How the three map to workRelevance is the only one you control directly and quickly — categories, services, description, on-site content. Prominence is the one you build slowly — reviews, links, mentions, real-world reputation. Distance is the one you cannot change without moving, which is why so much bad local advice is really an attempt to fake an address.

Local pillar 1 · The Business Profile

The profile is the ranked object in the local pack and the record AI assistants read when someone asks for a recommendation. It is also the highest-leverage hour of work in local SEO: most of it is free, fast, and under your direct control.

What Google says the profile is worth

Google's own 2026 Business Profile playbook publishes the numbers it wants operators to act on: 7× more clicks for businesses with complete profiles, 5× more views for regularly updated profiles, 42% more direction requests when photos are present on Maps, and 96% of customers more likely to visit a business that displays its hours. Treat these as directional marketing figures rather than controlled experiments — but note that they are Google telling you exactly which fields it wants populated.

Categories: the single biggest pack lever

The primary category was scored the number-one local pack factor in the 2026 expert survey. Google's guidance is to "select the fewest categories possible," using the test "this business IS a" rather than "HAS a" — categories describe what the business is, not what it offers or what is nearby. One primary plus up to nine secondary is the structural limit. Choosing a broader category than your competitors is usually a downgrade; choosing a more specific one that matches the query is usually the win.

The business name rule, and the uncomfortable tension

Google's rule is verbatim: "Your name should reflect your business's real-world name, as used consistently on your storefront, website, stationery, and as known to customers." Explicitly prohibited: marketing taglines, store codes, trademark symbols, phone numbers, URLs, all-caps words, hours, unsupported special characters, service or product descriptors, and location information.

Read this before you "just add the city"Keywords in the GBP business title were scored the third strongest local pack factor by the 2026 expert panel — and are simultaneously a documented guideline violation. Both statements are true. The tactic works, competitors can and do report it through the redressal process, and the downside is not a ranking dip but a suspended listing and zero local visibility while you appeal. The defensible version: if your legal or trading name genuinely contains the descriptor, use it consistently everywhere — signage, invoices, website, citations — so it is your real-world name rather than a keyword insertion.

The description

Not a pack ranking factor, and for years correctly dismissed as low priority. That has changed for one reason: it is the only field where the business describes itself in prose, and AI assistants answering local questions lift prose. The field rules are hard constraints — 750 characters maximum, only about the first 250 shown before "More," no URLs, no phone numbers or email addresses, no prices or promotional offers, no keyword stuffing or all-caps.

The structure that survives being quotedOpen with a plain semantic triple: [business name] provides [specific services] to [named service area]. Then expand — the specific services rather than category abstractions, the flagship offerings, the actual cities or counties served, and the customer type. Write the first 250 characters as if nothing after them will ever be read, because for most viewers that is true.

Local pillar 2 · Proximity and footprint

Distance is the factor with the largest effect and the least room for manoeuvre. Almost every local SEO scandal — virtual offices, fake addresses, lead-generation listings — is an attempt to cheat this one factor. What follows is what you can legitimately do instead.

Why "what's my ranking?" is the wrong question

Proximity of the address to the point of search was scored the second strongest local pack factor in 2026, and "physical address in the city of search" the fourth. Because the searcher's location is an input, your position is a surface across a map, not a number. A single-point rank check tells you almost nothing; a grid of checks across the area you actually serve tells you where your visibility ends — which is the real, actionable metric.

Service-area businesses: the rules that carry suspensions

Google's guidance for businesses that travel to customers: one profile for the central office, with a designated service area, staffed during business hours, and no displayed street address. Service areas "shouldn't extend beyond approximately 2 hours' driving time," and the profile playbook caps them at 20 designated areas. P.O. boxes and virtual offices are explicitly unacceptable.

The three highest-risk local mistakesThe 2026 expert survey scored suspension risk separately, and the top three negatives are all footprint problems: showing an address for a service-area business (risk score 105), overlapping service areas across multiple profiles (102), and an excessive service-area radius (82). These are not ranking penalties. They are listing removals.

Multiple locations, done legitimately

A separate profile per staffed, distinct physical location is correct and expected. A separate profile per service you offer at one location is not — Google's guidelines on departments and practitioners exist to prevent exactly that, and the 2026 developments round-up flagged auto dealers creating separate listings for oil changes and tire rotations as a live enforcement risk. The legitimate route into a new city is a real, staffed location with its own address, phone, hours, landing page and citation set.

Local pillar 4 · On-site local relevance

The website is where localised organic rankings are won, and it is the supporting evidence Google uses to judge what your Business Profile is relevant for. The two top-scored local organic factors in 2026 are both site-side and both structural.

A page per service, not a page listing services

"Dedicated page for each service" was scored the strongest local organic factor of 2026, ahead of geographic keyword relevance and link authority. A single "Services" page covering nine offerings competes for none of them properly. Each money page needs the substance a buyer actually wants: what the job involves, price ranges, how long it takes, credentials and licensing, photos of real work, and the questions people ask before booking.

Location pages that aren't doorways

City pages are where local SEO most often collides with Google's spam policies. Doorway abuse is defined as fabricating multiple pages or sites targeting specific searches to funnel visitors through intermediate pages — which is precisely what a template with the city name swapped is. The distinction that holds up is whether the page could only have been written about that place: real projects completed there, named neighbourhoods, local pricing or permitting differences, staff who work that territory, photos taken there, reviews from customers there.

The scaling testIf you can generate the page by find-and-replace, so can Google's classifiers. Twenty genuinely local pages outperform two hundred templated ones, and carry none of the scaled-content-abuse risk.

The link between profile and page

"Keywords in the GBP landing page title tag" was scored the fourth strongest local organic factor. The landing page you attach to the profile should be the most relevant page for what the profile is about — for a single-location business, usually the homepage; for a multi-location business, always that location's own page, never the generic homepage and never a tracking redirect.

Structured data

Google's LocalBusiness markup requires only name and address, but the recommended set is where the value sits: openingHoursSpecification, telephone, url, geo (five decimal places minimum), and priceRange. Multi-department businesses mark up each department separately. As with all structured data, it must describe what is visible on the page — Google warns that violations can draw a manual action.

The location page framework

Location pages fail for one of two opposite reasons: they're built as a doorway — a template with the city swapped, existing only to rank — or they're built as a brochure with no idea what job they're supposed to do for the visitor. This is a reverse-engineered framework: what the page is for, what it has to answer before a visitor will trust it, the layout that recurring studies converge on, and the conversion mechanics specific to local intent.

Purpose — what job the page actually does

A location page has three jobs running at once, and losing sight of any one of them is where pages fail:

1

Wins the search

Captures "[service] in [city]" and "near me" queries the homepage and a generic services page can't compete for — because Google's own systems model site and page focus by topic and geography, and a page that only vaguely mentions a place loses to one built around it.

2

Proves the presence

Is the on-site evidence that corroborates the Business Profile — the landing page a GBP listing should point to, and the entity data (NAP, hours, service area) Google cross-checks against citations and the profile itself.

3

Closes the visitor

Most location-page traffic has high, narrow intent — "does this business serve me, right now" — and converts or bounces in seconds. A page optimised for rankings but not for that decision wastes the traffic it earns.

Note what's not a job: ranking for the city name alone, or existing as inventory to make the site look comprehensive. A location page that isn't earning visits or producing calls is a doorway page wearing a disguise, whatever the intent behind it.

What it must answer

Before writing a word of copy, the page has to answer six questions a real visitor is silently asking. Every layout decision below exists to answer one of these, in order:

  • Do you serve me? — the place name, confirmed immediately, not inferred from a URL slug.
  • Are you actually here, or is this a funnel? — real address or defined service area, real hours, a page that couldn't be about anywhere else.
  • Can you do the specific thing I need? — the services available at or from this location, not a generic "we do everything" claim.
  • Why you and not the next result? — local proof: reviews from this area, work completed here, staff who cover this territory.
  • What does it cost and how fast can you start? — even a range or a "starts at" figure beats forcing a call to find out.
  • How do I act on this right now? — one obvious next step, reachable without hunting.
The RicketyRoo framing"Legitimate pages provide real-world value to users in that specific location, while spammy ones prioritize search algorithm manipulation over user experience." Every section below is answerable only if that's true of the business — the framework can't manufacture a local presence that doesn't exist.

Local pillar 5 · Citations and data consistency

Citations are the most over-sold service in local SEO and, in 2026, the one whose rationale changed most. They are worth less as a ranking factor than they were, and more as a supply of machine-readable corroboration.

The decline, and the twist

The 2026 expert survey continues a long trend of citation signals losing weight for local pack rankings — the category slipped again, and external linking to authority sites fell 64 positions. Buying a thousand directory listings has not been a serious strategy for years. But the same report notes that in AI search contexts "citations are back," on the reasoning that "mentions are the new link": generative systems corroborate facts about a business by finding them repeated consistently across independent sources. The value has moved from ranking weight to entity confirmation.

What that means practicallyStop measuring citations by count. Measure them by consistency and by whether the source is one a person or a model would actually consult: the major aggregators, the platforms with real traffic in your vertical, your chamber and trade bodies, your suppliers' dealer locators. Twenty accurate, relevant listings beat a thousand purchased ones, and the thousand create a maintenance liability that outlives the contract.

Local pillar 6 · Local authority and links

Prominence is the half of Google's model that money cannot shortcut. Google names links and reviews as its examples, and the 2026 survey puts inbound link quality third and industry-relevant link quantity fifth for local organic.

Two dimensions that matter more locally than nationally

For a local business, a link's value is roughly the product of two things: topical relevance (is the linking site in or adjacent to your industry?) and geographic relevance (is it in or about your area?). A link from the regional newspaper or the county trade association outperforms a higher-authority but placeless generic blog. This is the rare case where the small, obscure link is the better one.

The sources that reliably work

  • Sponsorships of real local things — teams, festivals, charities, school programmes — where a listing page results.
  • Chamber of commerce, BID, trade association and licensing body directories.
  • Local press: offering genuine expert comment, local data, or a story that concerns the area.
  • Supplier and manufacturer dealer locators, franchise directories, and certification listings you already qualify for.
  • Partnerships with non-competing local businesses that serve the same customer.
  • Local scholarships, community resources, and genuinely useful local guides that others cite.

All of these are structurally the same move: participate in the place, then let the link be a by-product. That also happens to be the only form of link building that survives Google's link spam policy, which prohibits links "created primarily for ranking manipulation, including paid links without proper attributes."

Local authority checklist
Prioritise industry-relevant links C
Scored a top-five local organic factor. Relevance to your trade beats raw authority for local queries.
Claim every membership and certification listing you already have D
The cheapest local links in existence: trade bodies, licensing registers, insurers, suppliers, franchisors. Most businesses have several unclaimed.
Sponsor something real, once a quarter D
Budget it as marketing, not link building. The link is the receipt for participation that would be worth doing anyway.
Become the quotable local expert D
Local journalists need sources on trade topics — energy costs, storm damage, regulation changes. Being reachable and quick is most of the job.
Publish something only a local operator could publish D
Your own pricing data, job volumes by season, permit timelines by council. Local originality earns links that generic advice never will.
Keep paid placements attributed A
Sponsored directory features and paid local guides need rel="sponsored". Paid links without attribution are link spam by definition.
Audit competitors' local links quarterly D
In a defined geography the pool of available local links is small and largely visible. Competitor profiles are effectively a to-do list.

Local pillar 7 · Behaviour and conversion

Behavioural signals rose alongside reviews as the biggest movers in the 2026 survey — and in the AI-answer context, click-through rate was scored the single most influential factor, ahead of its weight in the traditional local pack.

The signals in play

Click-through rate on the listing, whether the business is open at the moment of search, the Popular Times data attached to the profile, recency of uploaded video, and reactions to photos and reviews all scored highly in 2026 — the last two at 99–100 for conversion impact, alongside frequency of owner or manager activity at 92. Whether Google uses each of these directly is unproven. What is not in doubt is that they all correlate with a profile that a real, operating business actively maintains, and that is the thing being modelled.

The conversion path is narrowing

Sterling Sky's 2026 analysis of 179 profiles across 34 US law firms found click-to-call rates declining over two years, concentrated on mobile — the same period in which AI local packs began appearing without call buttons and paid units expanded into the space. The strategic response is not to chase the call button but to widen the paths: messaging, booking links, forms, and a mobile site that converts the visits you still get.

Behaviour & conversion checklist
Optimise the listing for the click, not just the rank C
Photos, rating, review count, hours, attributes and category all decide whether a ranked listing is the one chosen. CTR scored highest of all AI-visibility factors in 2026.
Be open when demand happens C
"Open at time of search" is a top-five pack factor. Extended or emergency hours, accurately listed, are a ranking decision as much as an operations one.
Offer more than one way to contact you D
Call buttons are disappearing from AI local packs. Messaging, booking links and a fast mobile form are the hedge.
Answer fast D
Local intent is immediate — a large share of consumers decide within minutes. Response speed is a ranking-adjacent business process.
Keep visible owner activity constant D
Replies, photos, posts, updated services. Frequency of owner activity scored 92 for conversion impact and is the clearest "this business is real and operating" signal available.
Fix the landing page before buying more visibility D
Mobile speed, click-to-call, prices or ranges, service area stated, proof. Extra impressions into a weak page is the most expensive way to learn this.

The 2026 AI local shift

This is the section with the shortest shelf life and the largest current impact. Local results changed shape in 2025–26 in ways that break the traditional relationship between ranking and revenue.

AI local packs

Google began rolling out AI-generated local packs on mobile in the US. Tracking through early 2026 found them on roughly 7–8% of tracked keywords, showing one to two businesses instead of three, frequently without call buttons, and often featuring different businesses than the traditional pack for the same query. The coverage difference is the striking figure: AI local packs surfaced 5,943 unique businesses where traditional 3-packs surfaced 18,330 — about 32% as many — with 88% of analysed markets showing a decline in the total number of visible businesses.

Paid encroachment

Over the same period, Local Pack Ads went from about 1% of tracked results in January 2025 to 22% by December 2025, and Local Services Ads from 11% to 31%. Organic local real estate shrank while AI units took the top of it.

Query fan-out

AI systems decompose a single question into multiple sub-queries and assemble an answer from all of them. One consequence reported in early 2026: holding a top-ten organic position gives roughly a 25% chance of appearing in the AI-generated result for that query. Ranking is becoming a probability of inclusion rather than a guarantee of placement.

Where consumers are going

BrightLocal's 2026 data shows generative AI tools now third among sources consumers use for local recommendations at 45% usage, up from 6%, with 40% saying they trust AI platforms for business recommendations and 82% reading AI-generated review summaries. Google's share of local discovery fell from 83% to 71% year over year. Separate tracking puts Google at about 73% of all searches with ChatGPT at roughly 2.8% — so this is a redistribution at the margin, not a collapse.

What this does and doesn't changeIt does not introduce a new set of fundamentals. Every input AI local answers draw on — profile completeness and accuracy, category, review volume and recency, consistent mentions across independent sources, a real website that says plainly what you do and where — is already in pillars one through seven. What it changes is the payoff structure: fewer businesses shown, fewer clicks per impression, and a much higher penalty for being the business whose data is thin, stale or inconsistent.

Local spam, suspensions and risk

Local is the one area of SEO where the penalty for policy violations is not a ranking drop but deletion — and where competitors are actively incentivised to report you. Risk management is a fundamental here, not an afterthought.

The asymmetry

An aggressive on-page tactic that fails costs you some rankings. A suspended Business Profile costs you the entire local channel while you appeal, with reviews, photos and history in limbo. Because Google accepts third-party edit suggestions and redressal reports, the people most motivated to notice your violation are the businesses ranking below you.

What actually triggers suspensions

  • Address problems — displaying an address as a service-area business, virtual offices, PO boxes, co-working desks, residential addresses shown when they shouldn't be.
  • Overlapping or oversized service areas across multiple profiles, or beyond roughly two hours' drive.
  • Business name violations — appended keywords, cities, taglines, phone numbers or URLs.
  • Listings that aren't businesses — one profile per service, per department, or per practitioner where the rules don't allow it.
  • Review manipulation — incentives, gating, staff-written or purchased reviews, multiple accounts.
  • Category or content that doesn't match reality, including websites that don't represent the individual location.
If you inherit a client with violationsFix quietly and in order: name first, then address display, then service areas, then duplicate listings. Changing several high-risk fields at once is itself a trigger for review. Document the real-world evidence — signage photos, utility bills, licences — before you need it, because reinstatement is an evidence process, not an argument.

Two measurement problems, one principle: report the thing that survives a SERP layout change. For organic that means business outcomes rather than rankings; for local it means actions on a map rather than a position at a point.

Part One · Measuring organic search

Part Two · Measuring local search

Local measurement has one structural problem — proximity makes position location-dependent — and one 2026 problem: rankings and revenue have partially decoupled. Both push in the same direction, toward measuring actions rather than positions.

Rankings are a surface, not a number

Because distance is an input, your position varies across your service area. A grid of checks across the territory you actually serve produces the only honest picture: where you are visible, where visibility ends, and whether that boundary is moving. A single rank check at the office address flatters everyone.

The decoupling

Search Engine Land's 2026 analysis of the problem is blunt: rank trackers show stable top-three positions while calls and site visits fall, because AI packs show fewer businesses, remove call buttons, and pick different winners, while ads take the space above. The recommendation follows directly — measure clicks-to-call and website clicks from the profile, form submissions, booked jobs, and revenue by landing page, not position.

Myths and low-yield work

Everything below is either explicitly contradicted by Google, unsupported by evidence, or true in a much narrower way than commonly claimed. Cutting these frees the time the real fundamentals need — and in the local half, several of them carry suspension risk rather than just wasted effort.

Universal search myths

"There's an ideal word count."
No. Google: "There's no magical word count target, minimum or maximum." Length correlates with rankings only because thorough answers tend to be longer. Padding a page to hit 2,000 words adds tokens Google may truncate anyway.
"Heading order is a ranking factor."
No. Google: "From Google Search perspective, it doesn't matter if you're using them out of order." Semantic order matters for screen readers, which is reason enough to do it — but it is an accessibility practice, not a ranking one.
"Meta keywords still help a little."
No. "Google Search doesn't use the keywords meta tag." Unchanged for well over a decade.
"Put keywords in the domain."
Almost nothing. Keywords in a domain have "hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs," and Google runs an exact match domain system specifically to stop such domains "receiving excessive ranking credit."
"Duplicate content carries a penalty."
No penalty exists. Google: having content at multiple URLs is "inefficient, but it's not something that will cause a manual action." The real cost is split signals and Google choosing a canonical you didn't intend.
"E-E-A-T is a ranking factor you can optimise."
Not directly. Asked whether E-E-A-T is a ranking factor, Google's answer is "No, it's not." It is the concept quality raters evaluate; ranking systems approximate it through many separate signals. Optimise the underlying reality — authorship, sourcing, accuracy, reputation — not the acronym.
"Domain Authority / Domain Rating is a Google metric."
No. DA and DR are third-party estimates built from link graphs. The leak shows Google has its own internal siteAuthority, which is not the same thing and cannot be read from any tool. Useful as relative benchmarks; useless as targets.
"Bounce rate and time-on-site from Analytics feed rankings."
Not as such. Google does not ingest your Analytics. What the leak shows is search-result click behaviour — a different thing measured on Google's side of the click. Improving genuine satisfaction affects both; gaming Analytics metrics affects neither.
"Publish more and traffic follows."
Volume alone fails. 96.55% of pages get zero organic traffic. Scaled publication without per-page value is also a named spam policy ("scaled content abuse"). Depth in a defined topic beats breadth across many.
"Keyword density / LSI keywords."
Neither is real. "LSI keywords" is a misapplication of an unrelated 1980s information-retrieval technique. Keyword density has no target; overdoing it triggers a documented spam policy. Google's systems match concepts, not term frequencies.
"Submit your site to search engines / pay to be indexed."
Unnecessary and impossible respectively. Google finds pages primarily "through links from other pages it already crawled," and "doesn't accept payment to rank pages higher."
"A perfect Lighthouse score will lift rankings."
Marginal at best. Core Web Vitals are assessed on field data at the 75th percentile, and Google states it "always seeks to show the most relevant content, even if the page experience is sub-par." Fix genuinely bad experiences; stop optimising 92 to 98.
"Structured data boosts rankings."
It boosts eligibility and clicks, not position. Google's own documentation frames it entirely as understanding and rich-result appearance. The CTR gains are real; the ranking claim isn't made.

Local search myths

"Buy citations — it's the foundation of local SEO."
Outdated. Citation signals have declined for years in expert weighting, and bulk directory listings create maintenance liabilities. Consistency across a small set of relevant sources is what matters; volume is not.
"Add your city and service to your business name."
Effective and against the rules. Keywords in the title do score as a strong pack factor — and Google's naming rule prohibits location and service descriptors outright. Competitors report it, and the penalty is suspension, not a ranking dip.
"Rent a virtual office to rank in the next city."
No. P.O. boxes and virtual offices are explicitly unacceptable, and address violations are the highest-scored suspension risk in the field. The only legitimate route into a new city is a real staffed location.
"Expand your service area to rank across a wider radius."
It doesn't work that way. The service area field describes where you'll travel; it does not extend the proximity radius Google will rank you in. Oversized areas add suspension risk with no ranking upside.
"Add every category that vaguely applies."
Counterproductive. Google's instruction is to "select the fewest categories possible," using "this business IS a" rather than "HAS a." Diluted categories weaken the primary signal that scored #1 for pack rankings.
"Ask customers to mention the service and city in their review."
Prohibited. Google's contribution policy specifically bars requesting that reviews include particular details. Solicit reviews freely; don't script them.
"Offer a discount for a review."
Explicit violation. "Payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" in exchange for a review is named in the policy, as is removing or revising negative reviews for compensation.
"Filter unhappy customers before the review request."
Review gating is banned. Discouraging negative reviews while selectively soliciting positive ones is a named merchant violation — and it is also how a 4.9 rating stops being believed.
"Spin up a page for every city within 50 miles."
Doorway abuse. Templated location pages are exactly the pattern Google's doorway policy describes. Twenty pages with real local substance beat two hundred find-and-replace pages, at a fraction of the risk.
"We rank #1 for the whole city."
Not a coherent claim. Proximity means position varies across the map. Without a grid, "#1" means "#1 at the one point that was checked" — often the office.
"Posting to GBP every week lifts rankings."
Weak as a ranking claim. Google recommends weekly posts and reports higher views for updated profiles, which is a good reason to post — but the evidence supports engagement and freshness, not pack position.
"You can pay Google for better local placement."
Directly denied. "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." Ads buy ad placement, which is a separate, clearly labelled surface — and one that expanded considerably in 2025–26.
